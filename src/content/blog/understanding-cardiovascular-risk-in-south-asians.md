Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, but for individuals of South Asian descent (including individuals with heritage from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka), the statistics are particularly alarming.

Despite making up approximately 25% of the world's population, South Asians account for an estimated **60% of the world's heart disease burden**. Furthermore, cardiac events frequently occur at younger ages—often 5 to 10 years earlier than in Western counterparts—and at lower body mass index (BMI) thresholds.

## Why Are South Asians at Higher Risk?

Medical researchers and cardiologists have identified several intersecting factors that contribute to this heightened susceptibility:

### 1. Metabolic & Genetic Predispositions
South Asians tend to store visceral fat—fat surrounding vital organs such as the liver and pancreas—even when overall body weight appears normal. This phenotype, often referred to as the *"thin-fat"* phenotype, is closely linked to:
- **Insulin resistance** and early-onset type 2 diabetes.
- **Atherogenic dyslipidemia**: elevated triglycerides, low HDL ("good" cholesterol), and high levels of small, dense LDL particles.
- Elevated **Lipoprotein(a)**, an independent, genetically inherited cardiovascular risk factor that is especially prevalent in South Asian communities.

### 2. Dietary Patterns
Traditional South Asian diets, while rich in plant-based ingredients and spices, frequently include high levels of refined carbohydrates (such as white rice, refined flours, and sugary sweets) and cooking mediums high in saturated or trans fats (like palm oil and reheated ghee).

### 3. Underestimated Risk by Standard Calculators
Conventional risk scores, such as the standard Framingham Risk Score or traditional ASCVD calculators, regularly **underestimate** actual cardiovascular risk in South Asian patients because they do not factor in unique regional biomarkers or earlier onset patterns.

## Proactive Steps You Can Take

Prevention is possible and highly effective when started early. Here are core recommendations from the DilSe clinical initiative:

1. **Get Screened Early**: Begin regular cholesterol, blood pressure, and HbA1c screenings by age 20 to 25 if you have a family history of premature heart disease.
2. **Ask About Advanced Lipid Markers**: Discuss checking **ApoB** and **Lipoprotein(a)** with your physician at least once in your adult life.
3. **Incorporate Aerobic & Resistance Training**: Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity and two sessions of strength training per week to combat insulin resistance.
4. **Make Culturally Tailored Dietary Adjustments**: Substitute whole grains (quinoa, brown basmati, millets) for refined grains, boost legumes and lean proteins, and use heart-healthy oils like cold-pressed olive or mustard oils in moderation.
